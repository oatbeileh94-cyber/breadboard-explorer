What to notice

  • Each numbered column holds two separate strips of five holes: a–e and f–j.
  • The center gap splits every column. Chips sit across it so each pin gets its own strip.
  • Holes in the same lettered row, like a5 and a6, are not connected.
  • The power rails run the long way along the edges: red for +, blue for −.
  • The top and bottom rails are separate. When you need power on both, join them with jumper wires: + to +, − to −.
  • Some boards break each rail partway along. Turn on Split power rails to see one.

Keyboard: Tab to the board, move with the arrow keys, press Enter to pin a hole and Esc to clear it. Turn on See inside the board to show the metal strips under the plastic.

Point at an empty hole to read its voltage, measured from the battery's − terminal. Point at a part to see the current through it. Pin one hole, then point at another to measure between the two.

    Ohm's law and the resistor color code

    Name Date

    Part AOhm's law

    Voltage pushes current through a resistance. Ohm's law ties the three together: V = I × R. Pick the quantity you want to find, set the other two, and follow the working.

    The circuit

    A battery (V) drives current (I) through a resistor (R). The ammeter (A) is connected in series, so all of the current passes through it. Thicker red arrows mean more current.

    The water picture

    Water picture of the same circuit: a tank, a tap and the water that flows out.

      Where the water picture breaks down
      • In a circuit, the current goes round a closed loop and back to the battery. From a tap, the water leaves and ends up in the sink. A closer match is a pump pushing water round a loop of pipe: the pump is the battery and a narrow section of pipe is the resistor.
      • The tap doesn't use up the water, and a resistor doesn't use up current: the same current flows in and out of it. What the tap does use up is the push: the water leaves it at a lower pressure. In the same way, the voltage drops across a resistor, and that energy turns into heat.
      • Careful with the words: a closed tap stops the water, but an open switch stops the current.
      • Voltage is a difference, like the height of the water surface above the tap. That's why a voltmeter always measures between two points.
      • A real tank drains, so its level and the flow slowly fall. A battery or bench supply holds its voltage steady, like a tank that is kept topped up.
      • A real tap doesn't follow Ohm's law exactly: doubling the water height gives only about 1.4 times the flow. A resistor does: double the voltage and you double the current.
